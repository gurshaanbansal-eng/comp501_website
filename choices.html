Societal Responses & Decisions Around Social Media Algorithms

Choices

This section explores the choices available to individuals, companies, and governments when responding to the impacts of social media algorithms. Content researched and written by Robert George William Sevele.

Government Regulation

Governments have now started to address social media algorithm impact, the most serious action so far coming from the European Union, where the Digital Services Act means that large platforms must carry out an algorithmic audit, give users an option for non-personalized feeds, and declare what makes content appear highly ranked (European Commission, 2026). The United States has already drafted legislation such as the Kids Online Safety Act that aims to address algorithmic harm caused to children and, in some countries, the proposed banning of platforms such as TikTok seems to be directly targeting the perceived influence of algorithms and threats to national security. The problem for regulation is finding the balance between free speech and innovation, on one hand, and demonstrable public harm, on the other.

Opt-Out & Algorithmic Transparency

The platforms have added small transparency tools like the "why am I seeing this?" option or to see the feed in chronological order. The EU has pushed it further: those platforms that have over 45 million monthly active users need to offer one recommender system not based on profiling (so the user will decide more what to see) (European Commission, 2026). Additionally to that researchers and campaigners demanded independent algorithm audits or standardized disclosures that would give sight of the ranking and promotion of content but would not reveal trade secrets.

Platform Accountability

Platforms have mostly governed themselves via internal content policies, with varying degrees of success. Under the DSA, a framework is established where the largest platforms must identify and assess systemic risks, defined by the European Commission as those that negatively impact fundamental rights, electoral processes and minors' mental well-being (2026). Entities such as Facebook’s Oversight Board seem to be formal mechanisms to encourage accountability, although critics claim that they hold little real power over platforms. Whistleblowers such as Frances Haugen have leaked internal research which demonstrates the continued disparity between public pronouncements of platforms and internal data, and the algorithmic harms that they cause.

Individual Choices

But users aren't completely powerless; curating carefully who you follow, choosing chronological feeds where options exist and refraining from interaction with provocative content, for example, will impact one's algorithmic experience significantly. Children will be banned from seeing personalised advertisements on the platforms and deceptive design techniques encouraging user behaviour are now prohibited under the DSA giving people baseline protections regardless (European Commission, 2026). Certain users have abandoned existing platforms to take up options such as Mastodon that are built without optimisation for engagement in mind. Ultimately, digital literacy- knowledge that your feed is engineered rather than naturally occurring- is perhaps the most basic tool users have.

The Path Forward

One answer isn't enough. The majority of professionals are likely inclined towards a combination of regulation by the government (setting minimum standards), transparency and accountability on the platform itself, and individual empowerment (via education, tools, and available information). The DSA goes quite some way to address this, streamlining 27 national pieces of legislation into one framework, imposing the greatest standards on the biggest platforms, and increasing platform accountability (European Commission, 2026). But how to do it on such a massive scale and over so many platforms.
